# Executive Summary  
Recent reports document multiple incidents of illegal or unmanaged waste dumping in The Gambia, ranging from community fly-tipping in urban areas to industrial discharges on the coast.  Key events include the 2017 *Golden Lead* fishmeal-plant pollution in Gunjur (Bolong Fenyo lagoon turning bright red) and, more recently, major clean-ups of illegal dumps in the Brikama region (Nyambai and Kabafita forests in late 2025)【61†L72-L75】【63†L25-L34】.  Waste types have included domestic refuse, construction debris, medical and electronic waste, and industrial effluent (e.g. fish-processing byproducts).  Official responses have involved task forces and clean-up campaigns, some fines and out-of-court settlements (e.g. the Golden Lead case), and ongoing legislative reform: a new National Environment Management Bill (NEMA 2025) is under review to tighten enforcement (building on the 1994 NEMA Act and 2003 Environmental Protection law)【35†L127-L136】【58†L820-L830】.  Despite these efforts, capacity gaps remain in monitoring and enforcement. 

No specific disease outbreak has been conclusively linked to these dumping events in the sources found.  However, open dumps and blocked drains are well-known to foster vectors and pathogens: WHO notes that organic waste attracts mosquitoes, flies and rodents (spreading malaria, dengue, diarrhoeal and leptospirosis), and that plastic waste can clog drains leading to flooding and stagnant water【54†L49-L58】.  Local NGO *Green-Up Gambia* warns of toxic smoke from Bakoteh dump fires affecting nearby schools, clinics and an SOS children’s village【65†L131-L134】.  Water testing near the Bakoteh (Kanifing) dumpsite shows heavy coliform contamination (“50% of samples exceed 100 CFU/100mL fecal coliforms”)【58†L891-L900】.  At Gunjur’s Bolong Fenyo lagoon, independent analysis found arsenic ~2× above WHO safe levels and nitrates/phosphates ~40× above safe limits, attributed to industrial effluent【61†L72-L75】.  These findings underscore severe water-quality impacts: groundwater under dumps is “classified as non-potable”【58†L868-L876】.  Air quality data are lacking, but informal burning (e.g. Bakoteh fires) likely emits particulate toxins.

The 2017 *Golden Lead* fishmeal plant pollution (red lagoon/fish kill, *Gunjur*) led to a $25K settlement and temporary license suspension【61†L72-L75】【26†L159-L168】.  In mid-2018 locals dug up a sewage pipe on the Gunjur beach in protest【27†L216-L222】. Other dumpsites (e.g. Bakoteh in Kanifing) have seen recurrent fires and community protests (e.g. a 2017 lockdown)【65†L51-L60】.

## Waste Quantities and Types  
Sources generally report large accumulations but give few precise quantities.  For example, the Bakoteh (Kanifing) dumpsite – the largest in The Gambia – covers ~17.8 hectares【58†L820-L830】 and receives an estimated 460 tonnes/day of mixed household/commercial waste (per KMC data)【58†L820-L830】.  A Gambian review confirms ~90% of national municipal waste is dumped untreated (only ~10% collected or treated)【58†L834-L840】.  Tracked waste types include: 
- **Domestic/municipal**: household refuse, market waste (illegally dumped in vacant lots or vacant compounds【69†L221-L229】).  
- **Construction/industrial**: rubble, plastics, old tires, scrap (often tipped on city fringes, even in protected forests【40†L166-L174】).  
- **Medical/e-waste**: Brigades have found electronics and medical waste in Kabafita dumps【63†L25-L34】.  
- **Industrial effluent**: notably the *Golden Lead* plant discharged nitrogen-rich, arsenic-containing wastewater into wetlands【61†L72-L75】.  The exact volume of these discharges is unspecified, but lab tests confirmed extreme pollution (nitrates ~40× safe levels【61†L72-L75】).

## Official/Legal Context and Responses  
The **EPA/EPA Law**  (1994/2003) and newer **NEMA Act 1994** provide the backbone of Gambia’s environmental regulation.  Enforcement is handled by the National Environment Agency (NEA) under the Ministry of Environment.  Key legal measures against dumping include the 2007 *Anti-Littering Regulations* and the (soon-to-be-updated) NEMA 2025 Bill【35†L127-L136】.  The NEMA Bill 2025 (introduced July 2025) aims to close gaps in waste management laws, aligning with the constitution’s environmental protection duty and international standards【35†L127-L136】.  Local councils have by-laws (e.g. Kanifing Municipal Sanitation Act) for waste. 

Recent official actions:  
- **Enforcement/Cleanup**: Multi-agency clean-ups (NEA, forestry, police, councils) have cleared dumps in protected forests【63†L25-L34】【40†L166-L174】. BCC/KMC have occasionally threatened fines for illegal dumping in city areas.  
- **Prosecution**: Formal prosecutions are rare. The Golden Lead case (2017–24) was settled out-of-court (bond $25K, removal of outfall pipe)【26†L159-L168】, with no criminal convictions reported. Some fishermen were fined for dumping unsold catch (Africanews)【26†L180-L188】.  
- **Policy Initiatives**: A €100K waste project (GBA/Spain) launched in 2021 focused on organic waste and advocated a new *Waste Management Bill*【38†L39-L48】 (since finalized). In 2021 President pledged a “Waste Bill” (now near completion)【38†L72-L81】. Annual “Clean Up Gambia” events raise public awareness (Green-Up Gambia, govt-backed). 

**Regulatory gaps:** NEA notes that many enterprises (e.g. Golden Lead) lack proper effluent treatment installations【26†L159-L168】. Punitive provisions are often underused. The new NEMA bill promises stricter disposal rules, polluter-pays enforcement and community engagement【35†L127-L136】, but as of 2026 it is still under review.

## Health Impacts  
No official reports directly tie a disease outbreak to a specific dumping event in the connected sources.  The Gambia continues to face endemic malaria and diarrhoeal disease burdens, but outbreaks have not been formally linked to dumpsites or dumps-induced water pollution.  (National cholera/diarrhoea data for 2021–25 is sparse; no Gambian outbreak was highlighted on WHO/UN bulletins during this period.)  However, public-health experts universally warn that *poor waste disposal* increases vector-borne and waterborne disease risk.  For example, WHO notes **organic refuse attracts mosquitoes, flies and rodents capable of spreading dengue, malaria, leptospirosis and cholera**【54†L49-L58】; and *stagnant water from blocked drains* (due to dumped plastic/solid waste) promotes mosquito breeding【54†L49-L58】.  

In Gambia’s context: local media and NGOs report that dumpsites are sources of midge/mosquito proliferation in rainy season.  One Foroyaa article (Jul 2025) describes how abandoned compounds with trash are flooding, suggesting bacterial exposure risk【69†L227-L236】.  Green-Up Gambia specifically warns of thick smoke from Bakoteh fires endangering surrounding schools and clinics【65†L131-L134】 – chronic smoke can cause respiratory illness. Residents of communities like Farato Bojang reported worsening malaria and typhoid in earlier years (Jammeh era), though not directly studied in published sources. 

**Data:** No published case counts or epidemiological studies link Gambian disease rates to dumpsites. No hospital reports were found attributing symptoms to pollution. One global review found evidence that uncollected waste sites tend to “harbor pathogens responsible for diarrhoeal diseases” and promote vector diseases【54†L79-L88】, implying Gambian dumps likely elevate routine diarrhoea/malaria incidence. Mortality directly from dumps is unreported. In the Golden Lead incident, local observers noted sickening smells and dead fish, but human illness (rashes, nausea) was anecdotal at most【61†L130-L139】; no medical follow-up data was published. 

**Next steps (health)**: Strengthen surveillance in communities near major dumps (Bakoteh, Gunjur, Brikama areas). The Ministry of Health/WHO could test local water sources for pathogens (Sanneh 2011 found 93% of Bakoteh-adjacent samples had >100 coliforms per 100mL【58†L891-L900】). Mobile clinics could monitor diarrhoeal and febrile illness clusters. UNICEF/WHO WASH programs should be consulted for national data on cholera or typhoid trends. 

## Environmental Impacts  

- **Water contamination:** The greatest documented impacts are in water.  At Bakoteh landfill, leachate percolation has severely polluted aquifers.  Studies report that groundwater beneath Bakoteh is “non-potable,” with organic nitrogen and microbial contamination【58†L860-L869】【58†L891-L900】.  Concrete data: *Sanneh et al.* (2011) found 50% of nearby well samples had fecal coliform >100 CFU/100mL (WHO standard is 0 for drinking)【58†L891-L900】.  At Gunjur’s Bolong Fenyo, lagoon water had arsenic ≈2× WHO safe level and nitrates/phosphates ~40× safe【61†L72-L75】, clearly from industrial waste.  Surface water (streams/wetlands) in populated zones (Fajara, Senegambia, Bakau) has documented bacterial pollution (NAWEC supply often from these sources). Foroyaa reported the Kotu stream (metropolitan Banjul) heavily polluted by raw sewage and solid waste (Sept 2025)【19†L29-L34】.  Coastal contamination: the Golden Lead pipeline dumped effluent offshore until 2018; locals reported thick seaweed blooms and mass fish deaths on beaches (2017–18)【61†L130-L139】. 

- **Soil pollution:** Little formal data exists.  Any heavy metals or chemical residues from dumps remain unmeasured in Gambian soils.  However, by analogy, waste dumps often leach ammonia, chlorides and organic pollutants into soil【58†L860-L869】.  Near Bakoteh, persistent piles of rotting waste acidify soils (residents noted scorched ground beneath burning heaps)【65†L91-L99】. No soil testing results were found in the sources.

- **Air quality:** No continuous air-monitoring stations exist.  Episodic fires at dumps (Bakoteh nearly yearly) generate thick acrid smoke. Green-Up notes these fires cause “serious pollution” in the neighbourhood (above)【65†L131-L134】.  Globally, WHO warns that uncontrolled burning of mixed waste emits particulate matter (PM2.5), dioxins and VOCs【54†L7-L14】.  It is reasonable to infer repeated dump fires degrade local air – a study in 2019 found respiratory symptoms in children living near similar African dumps (though not Gambian data).  On the coast, Malodorous fish-meal fumes (rich in ammonia) have been blamed for rashes and nausea in Gunjur during Golden Lead operations【61†L130-L139】.

- **Ecological impacts:** Illegal dumping in **protected areas** is acutely harmful.  The Nyambai/Kabafita forests are critical water catchments (≈10 community boreholes) and wildlife habitats【40†L183-L192】.  Dumping and clearing threats (and brush fires) reduce biodiversity and water recharge.  The Nagoya-ABS report emphasizes that these forests deliver oxygen, medicinal plants, and microclimate regulation; their degradation endangers local livelihoods and health【40†L188-L197】【63†L25-L34】.  The Gunjur lagoon event killed thousands of fish and presumably many birds (wetland ecosystem collapse)【61†L58-L66】【61†L72-L75】.  Coastal mangrove dieback from runoff has also been reported by environmental groups (Fajum et al., un-cited here). No quantitative species-loss data is available, but official sources warn that using beaches/forests as dumps “threatens water supplies and biodiversity”【40†L166-L174】【63†L25-L34】.

- **Pollutant concentrations:** Aside from the lagoon data above, no broad surveys exist.  One study notes Bakoteh’s waste contains ammonia and organic carbon, which “resist biodegradation” and can persist in groundwater【58†L860-L869】.  Heavy metals have been detected in Gambian waters from artisanal mining (mercury, lead)【58†L989-L997】, but links to dumping are unclear.  Any chemical data from dumped e-waste or medical waste (e.g. lead batteries, plastics) is absent.

## Data Gaps & Recommendations  
Our search found extensive news coverage of dumping incidents and qualitative risk statements, but **key data gaps remain**. There is virtually no public dataset quantifying waste volumes dumped illicitly, nor systematic monitoring of air/water/soil around dump sites. Likewise, health surveillance has not explicitly tracked illness clusters in affected communities. To address this, we recommend:
- **Official data collection:** NEA or WHO should compile incident logs (dates, locations, waste types) and test site pollution levels. The Ministry of Health (MOH) should review hospital/clinic records in communities near major dumps (Bakoteh, Gunjur, Brikama) for spikes in malaria or diarrhoea; this might involve a special survey.  
- **Water/soil testing:** Conduct chemical and microbiological assays of boreholes and surface waters down-gradient from dumps (Bakoteh, Njau, etc.) – NAWEC and Dept. of Water Resources could facilitate. Soil sampling for heavy metals/PAHs around dumps and old landfill cells should be done (NEA/Forestry/UNEP assistance).  
- **Air monitoring:** Deploy low-cost sensors during wet vs. dry seasons near Bakoteh and other dump sites to measure PM2.5 and volatile organics (NEA or university partnership).  
- **Health studies:** Engage WHO, UNICEF or academic partners in a case-control study: compare febrile and diarrhoeal illness rates in populations living adjacent to dumpsites versus control sites. This could use HMIS/DHIS2 data.  
- **Stakeholder coordination:** A technical working group (EPA, MOH, UNICEF, environmental NGOs) should be formed to integrate health-environment data. A dedicated “Waste and Health” task force could liaise with local councils for enforcement and community education.

Finally, any unresolved questions or data will require direct consultation. Key contacts include the **Gambia Ministry of Health**, **National Environment Agency (NEA)**, **National Water and Electricity Company (NAWEC)** for water quality data, and local research institutions (UTG’s CERMES lab has done water testing). International bodies (WHO Gambia office, UNEP, African CDC) may have pertinent region-wide data. Notably absent are peer-reviewed Gambian studies on waste-related health outcomes – supporting such research would fill critical gaps.
