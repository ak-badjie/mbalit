# `mbalit` — Pitch Script

**Tone:** calm but charged. Quiet anger underneath. Slow on names. Heavy on the human cost. Don't worry about length — read at your natural fast pace; the visuals will hold the screen as long as you need.

**Total runtime target:** ~10 minutes (4 min Act I · 2 min Act II · 4 min Act III + close)

---

## ACT I — The problem

### Scene 1 · The fields
> "Right now, while we're sitting in this room — there are children working in The Gambia's rubbish.
>
> Not as a metaphor. Not in some statistic from years ago. Today. This morning. On the Bakoteh dumpsite, ten minutes from where most of us live, children with bare feet are walking on broken glass and burnt plastic, looking for something they can sell for a few coins.
>
> This is the country we're standing in. And before we talk about anything else — we have to look at it."

*(Long pause. Let the photo sit.)*

---

### Scene 2 · Problem 1 of 4 — Water bodies *(read the full Bolong Fenyo beat — slow on it)*
> "Problem one. They dump it in our rivers.
>
> And I want you to really understand what that means. When waste sits in our rivers, it does not stay there politely. It poisons the water. It kills the fish. It strips the oxygen out until nothing lives in there anymore. The aquatic life — the same fish our fishermen depend on, the same water our cattle drink, the same lagoons our children swim in — we are quietly murdering all of it.
>
> And it does not even start at the river. It starts on the street. Every plastic bag, every bottle, every bit of household waste tossed into a corner — when the rains come, the water washes it. Out of the alley, out of the gutter, out of the neighborhood, straight into the river. The street becomes the river. The river becomes the dump.
>
> And when that waste is still sitting in our neighborhoods, in puddles, in choked drains — that standing water becomes a mosquito nursery. And those mosquitoes carry malaria. They carry dengue. They are the reason our children are sleeping under nets and still falling sick.
>
> *(Beat.)*
>
> May 2017. The Bolong Fenyo lagoon at Gunjur. Overnight, the water turned blood red. By morning the fish were floating. The birds that fed on those fish died too. An entire ecosystem suffocated in the space of a single night.
>
> When the lab report came back: nitrate levels were forty times the safe limit. Arsenic was double the WHO ceiling. And in nearby Bakoteh — half of the wells the community drinks from already have fecal coliform above one hundred CFU per hundred millilitres.
>
> *(Beat.)*
>
> Forty times. Double. Half. These are not foreign numbers. This is the water under our feet."

---

### Scene 3 · Problem 2 of 4 — Streets & neighbourhoods
> "Problem two. They dump it in our streets.
>
> Walk through any market in Greater Banjul and look at the corners. Look at the drains. Ninety percent of the waste this country generates is never treated. Only ten percent is even collected.
>
> The first hard rain turns the road into a sewer. The next dry week turns it into a mosquito nursery — and those mosquitoes are how malaria stays in this country. That is the line. From a piece of plastic in a drain, to a child in a hospital bed.
>
> And because nobody comes to take the rubbish away — what do people do? They light a match. They burn it. In their own compounds. In front of their own houses. Releasing CO2, releasing dioxins, releasing every poison that plastic was holding, straight into the air our children are breathing.
>
> So this is the situation: we cannot live with it on the ground, and we cannot live with it in the air. The only honest way out of this is recycling. Properly. At scale. As a system.
>
> But that is a journey. And every journey starts somewhere. It starts here — with somebody finally going to people's gates and taking the rubbish away."

---

### Scene 4 · Problem 3 of 4 — Open burning
> "Problem three — and then they set it on fire.
>
> Bakoteh dumpsite alone receives four hundred and sixty tonnes of waste every single day. Almost eighteen hectares of open ground, and most of it is burning at any given moment.
>
> The smoke does not stay over the dump. It drifts. It reaches schools. It reaches clinics. It reaches an SOS Children's Village sitting directly downwind. Children who already have nothing — and we have given them a permanent cloud of burning plastic to breathe.
>
> Every tonne we burn instead of process is more CO2 in the air, more carcinogens in our lungs, and more proof that we have to build the alternative. Because right now, fire is the only system we have."

---

### Scene 5 · Problem 4 of 4 — Forests & wildlife
> "Problem four. They dump it in our forests.
>
> Last December — December 2025 — multiple agencies had to come together to physically pull illegal dumps out of the Nyambai and Kabafita forests. These are not random patches of bush. These are water catchment forests. Ten community boreholes — ten village water supplies — were already at risk because of what was being dumped on top of them.
>
> Cattle now graze on plastic at Brufut. We have photographs of cows pulling polythene out of the dirt because there is nothing else for them to eat in places that used to be open pasture.
>
> And it is the same families every time. The mother whose well is contaminated is the same mother whose son is on the dumpsite at fifteen pence a bag. The father who can no longer fish in the lagoon is the same father whose daughter has stopped going to school because the family needs the income. We are watching one slow-motion injury hit the same households, from every direction at once."

---

### Scene 6 · The human cost
> "And it lands on the same families twice.
>
> Once — when their children scavenge instead of going to school. A full bag of recyclable metal — an entire day on that dumpsite — pays fifteen pence. Fifteen.
>
> And again — when the contaminated water and the smoke send those same children to a clinic. Ninety-three percent of the water samples taken adjacent to Bakoteh exceed the safe coliform limit.
>
> The same family. Hit twice. And nobody is coming to break the cycle. Until now."

---

### Scene 7 · Neneh's story *(slow down — this carries the whole act)*
> "This is Neneh.
>
> She was twelve years old. The third of nine children. She had not been to school in four years.
>
> She worked the Bakoteh dump alongside her brothers and sisters. Fifteen pence a bag. That was her contribution to her family.
>
> *(Beat.)*
>
> I want to be very clear about something. I personally do not believe — I do not believe — that any child should ever have to start working before they have had a basic education. That should not be controversial in any country in the world. And it certainly should not be controversial here.
>
> In 2015, Child Aid Gambia sponsored Neneh into Lower Basic School. By 2017 — two years later — she was scoring A grades. English became her favourite subject.
>
> *(Beat.)*
>
> Today she lives away from the dump. She never has to scavenge again. She is one girl. One sponsorship. One school place. One whole life rewritten.
>
> Her story is the proof that this is solvable. And it is the reason we built `mbalit`."

---

### Scene 8 · The voices *(read each name slowly — let the room feel them)*
> "These people have names.
>
> **Tida Sillah.**
> **Saffiatou Sambou.**
> **Adama Ida Ceesay.**
> **Mariama Nyassi.**
> **Ndey Ngum.**
>
> Mothers. Landlords. Waste pickers. Residents whose children have asthma because the dump won't stop burning. Homeowners who cannot sleep at night because of the flies and the smell. Women who take their children with them to scavenge, because the choice is between school and food and they have already had to choose.
>
> They have told their story to Voice Gambia. To The Point. To NGO field workers. They have been telling it for years. And nobody has built them anything.
>
> So — we will."

---

### Scene 9 · The vision
> "What if we turn this into an industry.
>
> Because this is genuinely how industries come into existence. You cannot talk about recycling, you cannot talk about plastic re-manufacturing, you cannot talk about a circular economy — until you have a system that reliably collects the waste in the first place.
>
> Collect. Then sort. Then recycle. Then goods.
>
> Civilization is built on what we recover from what we throw away. That is what `mbalit` is for."

*(Pause for the depth-blur transition into Act II.)*

## ACT II — The product (~12s per feature)

### Scene 10 · Feature 01 · Phone + PIN onboarding
> "No SMS. No verification code. Just a phone number and a four-digit PIN. Twelve seconds — and you have an account."

### Scene 11 · Feature 02 · Choose what you have
> "Household. Garden. Renovation. E-waste. The driver knows what they're picking up before they get there."

### Scene 12 · Feature 03 · Transparent pricing
> "The price ticks up live as you add buckets. No haggling at the gate."

### Scene 13 · Feature 04 · Plus Code pickup
> "Greater Banjul is full of compounds with no formal address. A Plus Code is a ten-by-ten metre square anyone with a phone can resolve."

### Scene 14 · Feature 05 · Live broadcast
> "The job goes out to every nearby driver — instantly."

### Scene 15 · Feature 06 · Race condition, solved
> "Two drivers tap *Accept* in the same second — only one gets the job. Atomic single-winner write. No double-booking. Ever."

### Scene 16 · Feature 07 · Live tracking
> "From the moment they accept, you watch them on the map. They watch you. Both phones, same truth."

### Scene 17 · Feature 08 · Mobile-money checkout
> "Wave. AfriMoney. QMoney. Yonna Wallet — the four wallets that already move money in The Gambia. The driver gets paid before they drive away."

### Scene 18 · Feature 09 · Hazard reporting
> "And here is where it stops being just a pickup app.
>
> See illegal dumping? See a pile of waste in a river? See a fire on a roadside? One photo. One tap. The Kanifing Municipal Council gets it the moment it lands — geo-tagged, time-stamped, undeniable.
>
> Because we are not just providing a way to *report* the problem. We are providing the system that closes the loop. Reports go to the people who actually have the authority to act on them. Action gets logged. Status gets returned to the citizen who reported it. And the next person who walks past that same spot can see whether anything was done.
>
> A report that vanishes into a folder is what we have today. What `mbalit` builds is accountability you can hold in your hand."

### Scene 19 · Feature 10 · Authority dashboard
> "And on the other side — the Kanifing Municipal Council sees every report in real time. Live map. Live counts. New, acknowledged, resolved. They can act on the same day — not the same quarter. The data they need to do their job is finally going to land on their desk the moment something happens."

## Stage directions — cheat sheet

- **Three slowest moments** — they carry the whole pitch:
  1. "Neneh is why we built this."
  2. The five names in Scene 8.
  3. "`mbalit` is that road."
- **Don't rush the stats.** Read each number. Let the audience absorb one before you give them the next.
- **Scene 2 (water) and Scene 7 (Neneh) are the longest beats.** Don't be afraid of them — the visuals will hold.
- **Hand the phone over *before* you start talking** in Scene 20 — physical action sells the demo more than the demo does.
- **The team beat (Alex / Uncle Rob / Mr. Collie)** — say each name on its own line. Look around the room. The silence between names does the work.
